The UK keeps four lists of buildings worth saving. Together they hold {{n_total|int}} entries. I put every one of them on one map, read their names to see what they are, and read their official descriptions to see when they were built.

Somebody decided each of these was worth keeping. Since the late 1940s the nations of the UK have listed buildings of special architectural or historic interest, and a listed building cannot be knocked down or much altered without consent. Historic England, Cadw in Wales, Historic Environment Scotland and Northern Ireland's Department for Communities each publish their list as open data. What the files do not say is what each building is, or when it was built. Those things are in the words.

So I read them. Each entry's name says, more often than not, what it is: a barn, a church, a telephone kiosk. Each entry's official description usually opens with its date: "Farmhouse. Late C17." I read the description of every one of Wales's {{nations.Wales.n|int}} entries, and random samples of {{dates.England.n_sampled|int}} in England and {{dates.Scotland.n_sampled|int}} in Scotland. Northern Ireland's record gives its own date bands.

Half a million dots

There are {{n_total|int}} entries in all, about {{uk_per_1000|f1}} for every 1,000 people. An entry is not always one building: it can be a whole terrace or a single gravestone. Wales has the most per person ({{nations.Wales.per_1000|f1}} per 1,000), then Scotland ({{nations.Scotland.per_1000|f1}}), England ({{nations.England.per_1000|f1}}) and Northern Ireland ({{nations["Northern Ireland"].per_1000|f1}}).

Among counties and unitary authorities, the tiny populations of the Isles of Scilly ({{county_top_per_head.0.per_1000|f1}} per 1,000) and the City of London ({{county_top_per_head.1.per_1000|f1}}) put them on top. Next come rural Wales and the Marches: Powys ({{county_top_per_head.2.per_1000|f1}}), Gwynedd and Rutland. At the bottom are outer London boroughs and twentieth-century towns: Barking and Dagenham has {{county_bottom_per_head.0.per_1000|f2}} entries per 1,000 residents.

Towns tell the same story at a finer grain. Among towns of 10,000 people or more in England and Wales, {{town_top_per_head.0.name|raw}} comes first with {{town_top_per_head.0.per_1000|f1}} entries per 1,000 residents, then {{town_top_per_head.1.name|raw}}, {{town_top_per_head.2.name|raw}} and {{town_top_per_head.3.name|raw}}. Among bigger places, {{town50_top_per_head.0.name|raw}} leads. In small places the numbers get silly: {{village_top_per_head.0.name|raw}}, a small harbour town of about {{village_top_per_head.0.pop|int}} people, has {{village_top_per_head.0.n|int}} entries.

Houses, barns and a great many churches

Read by their names, {{types.UK.house.pct|f1}}% of entries are houses and cottages, {{types.UK.farm.pct|f1}}% barns and farm buildings, {{types.UK.church.pct|f1}}% churches and chapels and {{types.UK.wall.pct|f1}}% walls, gates and railings. About a quarter have names that do not say. And there are oddities by the thousand: {{n_kiosk|int}} telephone kiosks, {{n_milestone|int}} milestones and mileposts, {{n_war_memorial|int}} war memorials.

The top grade is another world. Only {{uk_top_pct|f1}}% of all entries are in it. In England, {{top_church_pct.England|int}}% of Grade I entries are churches and chapels. In Wales castles make up {{types.Wales.defence.top_pct|int}}% of Grade I. Scotland's category A is led by houses.

Georgian and Victorian, mostly

The listed UK is mostly from the 1700s and 1800s. In England's sample, {{dates.England.dist.c18.pct|int}}% of dated entries were built in the 1700s and {{dates.England.dist.c19.pct|int}}% in the 1800s; {{dates.England.dist.c17.pct|int}}% in the 1600s and {{dates.England.dist.le15.pct|int}}% before 1500. England's list is the oldest of the four. Wales has a similar share from before 1500 but many more from the 1800s ({{dates.Wales.dist.c19.pct|int}}%). Scotland's is younger again ({{dates.Scotland.dist.c19.pct|int}}% from the 1800s) and Northern Ireland's youngest ({{dates["Northern Ireland"].dist.c19.pct|int}}%).

Inside England the oldest stock is in the east. In the East of England, {{dates.England.by_region["East of England"].le15.pct|int}}% of sampled entries date from before 1500 and {{dates.England.by_region["East of England"].c16.pct|int}}% from the 1500s. London's are the youngest: {{dates.England.by_region.London.c19.pct|int}}% from the 1800s.

Wales is the one place where I could date every entry, so it can be mapped in full. Entries with a date before 1500 are commonest in the Vale of Glamorgan ({{dates.Wales.by_council["Vale of Glamorgan"].le15.pct|int}}%) and Monmouthshire ({{dates.Wales.by_council.Monmouthshire.le15.pct|int}}%); in Ceredigion, in the west, {{dates.Wales.by_council.Ceredigion.c19.pct|int}}% date from the 1800s.

Waves of listing

Listing came in waves. {{list_years.England.in_1980s_pct|int}}% of England's entries were first listed in the 1980s, when the lists were resurveyed parish by parish; the busiest single year was {{list_years.England.peak_year|raw}}, with {{list_years.England.peak_n|int}} entries. Scotland's peak was {{list_years.Scotland.peak_year|raw}}, Wales's {{list_years.Wales.peak_year|raw}}. The newest wave is war memorials: of the {{eng_recent.n|int}} entries England has listed since 2015, {{eng_recent.types.war_memorial|int}} are war memorials, more than half, in the years around the centenary of the First World War.

How I did this

The four lists come from the publishers' own open data services, all under the Open Government Licence. Types come from simple keyword rules on each entry's name; where Cadw gives its own class, the rules agreed {{type_check_wales.agree_pct|int}}% of the time, so treat them as a guide. Dates come from the first date in each description. I read {{date_check.all.n|int}} descriptions to check the rules, and they were right {{date_check.all.right_pct|int}}% of the time where a date could be judged. The first date is usually the main build; for old churches it is sometimes a saint's foundation, so I don't name an "oldest listed building". Per head figures use ONS, NRS and NISRA populations. For the oldest buildings still standing, see my earlier study Still Standing.
