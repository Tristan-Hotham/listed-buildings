The Listed Kingdom

Every listed building in the United Kingdom on one map: {{n_total|int}} entries on the four national lists, from cathedrals and castles to telephone boxes and milestones. Where are they thickest? What gets listed? When were they built, and when did someone decide they were worth keeping?

Somebody decided each of these was worth keeping. Since the late 1940s the four nations of the UK have kept lists of buildings of special architectural or historic interest, and a listed building cannot be demolished or much altered without consent. Each list is published as open data: Historic England's National Heritage List, Cadw's list for Wales, Historic Environment Scotland's list and Northern Ireland's Historic Buildings Record. Put together they hold {{n_total|int}} entries.

An entry is not always one building. A single entry can be a whole terrace, a church, a farmhouse with its barn, a gravestone or a milestone. And the four lists were made by different people with different rules, so a Grade II building in Kent and a category B building in Fife are not quite the same thing. With those cautions, here they all are.

    03 · WhatHouses, barns and a great many churches

    The lists do not have a shared "type" field, so I read each entry's name with a set of open keyword rules (a "Barn at Lot Farm" is a barn; "The Bull Public House" is a pub). The rules give a type to {{types.UK.house.pct|f1}}% houses and cottages, {{types.UK.farm.pct|f1}}% barns and farm buildings, {{types.UK.wall.pct|f1}}% walls, gates and railings and {{types.UK.church.pct|f1}}% churches and chapels. A further {{types.UK.address.pct|f1}}% are numbered street buildings whose name is just an address, and {{types.UK.other.pct|f1}}% have names that do not say what they are ("The Laurels").

    There are {{n_kiosk|int}} listed telephone kiosks, {{n_milestone|int}} listed milestones and mileposts, and {{n_war_memorial|int}} listed war memorials.

Northern Ireland uses A, B+, B1 and B2; A is {{nations["Northern Ireland"].top_pct|f1}}%.

    04 · When builtGeorgian, Victorian and older

    None of the four open datasets has a "date built" field except Northern Ireland's. The date is in the words: each entry has a written description that usually starts with it ("Farmhouse. Late C17." or "1845, by Robert Dodds"). I read every one of Wales's {{nations.Wales.n|int}} Cadw descriptions, a random sample of {{dates.England.n_sampled|int}} of England's entries and {{dates.Scotland.n_sampled|int}} of Scotland's, and took the first date each one gives. Rules found a date in {{dates.England.dated_pct|int}}% of England's sampled descriptions, {{dates.Scotland.dated_pct|int}}% of Scotland's and {{dates.Wales.dated_pct|int}}% of Wales's.

    The listed UK is mostly Georgian and Victorian. In England's sample, {{dates.England.dist.c18.pct|int}}% of dated entries were built in the 1700s and {{dates.England.dist.c19.pct|int}}% in the 1800s; {{dates.England.dist.c17.pct|int}}% in the 1600s and {{dates.England.dist.le15.pct|int}}% before 1500. England's list is the oldest of the four. Wales has a similar share from before 1500 ({{dates.Wales.dist.le15.pct|int}}%) but far more from the 1800s ({{dates.Wales.dist.c19.pct|int}}%); Scotland is younger again ({{dates.Scotland.dist.c19.pct|int}}% from the 1800s) and Northern Ireland's record youngest of all ({{dates["Northern Ireland"].dist.c19.pct|int}}%).

    Inside England the difference is east and west, old and new. The East of England has the oldest listed buildings: {{dates.England.by_region["East of England"].le15.pct|int}}% from before 1500 and {{dates.England.by_region["East of England"].c16.pct|int}}% from the 1500s. London's are the youngest, {{dates.England.by_region.London.c19.pct|int}}% from the 1800s, and the North West's next ({{dates.England.by_region["North West"].c19.pct|int}}%).

    A warning about the very oldest dates. The first date in a description is usually the build date, but for old churches it is often the date of the site: Welsh descriptions open with a saint's foundation in the 600s, or a Roman camp, before saying that the present church is medieval. I skip a first date whose sentence speaks of a foundation or a site when a later date follows, but some remain, so I do not crown an "oldest listed building" from these rules. In England's sample the earliest dates belong to churches with fabric from before the Norman Conquest, such as St Mary's at Bampton in Oxfordshire ({{oldest.England_sample.0.match|raw}}). For the oldest buildings still standing, see my earlier study Still Standing, which maps every building in Wikidata dated before 1100.

    Listing has come in waves. In England, {{list_years.England.before_1970_pct|int}}% of today's entries were first listed before 1970, {{list_years.England.in_1980s_pct|int}}% in the 1980s, when the lists were resurveyed parish by parish, and {{list_years.England.since_2000_pct|int}}% since 2000. The busiest single year was {{list_years.England.peak_year|raw}}, with {{list_years.England.peak_n|int}} entries. Scotland's busiest year was {{list_years.Scotland.peak_year|raw}} and Wales's {{list_years.Wales.peak_year|raw}}; {{list_years.Wales.since_2000_pct|int}}% of Wales's entries were listed since 2000.

    The newest listings tell their own story. Of the {{eng_recent.n|int}} entries England has listed since 2015, {{eng_recent.types.war_memorial|int}} are war memorials, more than half, as the centenary of the First World War prompted a push to protect them.

    How I did this

    Lists. Historic England's National Heritage List for England (listed building points, {{nations.England.n|int}} entries), Historic Environment Scotland's listed buildings ({{nations.Scotland.n|int}}), Cadw's listed buildings for Wales ({{nations.Wales.n|int}}) and the Department for Communities' Historic Buildings Record for Northern Ireland, keeping only listed grades A, B+, B1, B2 and B ({{nations["Northern Ireland"].n|int}}; {{ni_dropped_record_only|int}} "record only" and delisted entries are left out). All were read from the publishers' own open data services on {{retrieved|raw}}.

    Types. Keyword rules in code/rules.py read each entry's name: the head of the name (before "at", "to", "of" or a comma) is searched for words like barn, church, kiosk or bridge, and the last such word wins, since English names end in their head noun. Northern Ireland's record gives a use, which the same rules read. As a check, Wales's list has its own broad class for each entry: where the rules gave a type, it was consistent with Cadw's class in {{type_check_wales.agree_pct|int}}% of {{type_check_wales.n|int}} entries ({{type_check_wales.agree_pct_excl_walls|int}}% leaving out walls and gates, which Cadw often files under the house they belong to). Read the types as a good guide, not a census.

    Dates. I read the description of every entry in Wales, a seeded random sample of entries in England and Scotland, and took the first date each description gives: a year ("1845", "c1800") or a century ("Late C17", "18th century"). A century alone is placed in its middle; "early" and "late" move it by 35 years. Where no number appears, a period word (Georgian, Victorian, medieval) is used and flagged. The first date is normally the date of the main build; older parts often come later in the text, so these dates lean young for buildings with long histories. The text is not republished, only the date and the words it came from. To test the rules I read {{date_check.all.n|int}} random descriptions (60 from England, 30 each from Scotland and Wales, as part of the AI-assisted analysis): of those that could be judged, the rules put {{date_check.all.right_pct|f1}}% in the right century ({{date_check.all.wrong|int}} wrong), and two of the three errors were then fixed in the rules. Wikidata's inception dates ({{wikidata.n|int}} entries) fill in some others on the map, marked as such; they lean towards old and famous buildings.

    People and places. Counties and unitary authorities: ONS December 2023 boundaries and mid-{{pop_year|raw}} estimates (with NRS and NISRA). Towns: ONS built-up areas (2022) and Census 2021 residents. England's regions: each entry takes the region of its nearest census area.

    Limits. An entry can cover one milestone or a whole terrace, so counts are of entries, not buildings. Grades differ between nations. Buildings demolished or delisted are gone from today's lists, so the listing waves are of survivors. A few points are placed at a grid reference rather than on the building.
